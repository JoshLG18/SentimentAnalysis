Slide 1 - Background
Okay, so here's the thing. Traditional financial models are all about numbers—stock prices, 
economic data, GDP, all that quantitative stuff. But there's a problem: they're missing emotion. 
News headlines shape how investors feel, and emotions literally drive market movements.

Now, people have studied social media sentiment before. Twitter vibes, Reddit posts, all that. 
But financial news headlines? That's actually been less researched, even though it probably matters
way more. It's more official, more trusted.

Here's where it gets interesting: traditional machine learning methods—like Logistic Regression—r
equire you to manually pick features. You have to decide what words matter. That's hard, especially
in finance where language is subtle and nuanced.

But deep learning? Models like LSTMs and Transformers can automatically learn what matters. 
They find patterns you wouldn't think to look for. And FinBERT—which is BERT trained specifically
on financial text—is even better. It already understands finance.

But here's my question: if FinBERT is already so good, does adding a more complex architecture
on top actually help? Or do you just get slightly better results for way more compute?

Slide 2 - Dataset Overview
For this project, I used data from a repository of large datasets, perfect for these neural models, 
called HuggingFace.
- The dataset contains news headlines from various sources, along with their corresponding
sentiment labels (positive, negative, neutral).

Here's the thing though: it's imbalanced. About 62.5% are neutral, 25% positive, 12.5% negative. 
That matters because models naturally get better at predicting the majority class. 
So right from the start, all models will be biased toward saying "neutral."

The task is straightforward: predict sentiment. Given a headline, classify it as positive, negative,
or neutral. That's multi-class classification.

Slide 3 - Methods
- When completing natural language processing, the first step is to transform the text data into 
a form that these networks can understand. The main way we did this was using FinBERT,
which is a pre-trained language model specifically designed for financial text. As well as using 
TF-IDF vectorization for baseline.

Next, is where the fun starts - the models!
- We experimented with several neural network architectures, including:
    - MLP (Multilayer Perceptron)
    - LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory)
    - and a Transformer model.

Each model gave a different approach to learning patterns in the text data.
but ultimately, outputed a sentiment prediction for each headline.

Slide 4 - Models

- Starting with a simple 3 Layer MLP, regularised using dropout and layer normalisation.
- Next, we had the bidirectional LSTM model with attention, which is designed to handle sequential data like text.
- Finally, we had the Transformer model, which uses self-attention mechanisms to capture relationships in the text more effectively.
- As you can see the models get more complex as we go along, but does that mean better performance?

Slide 5 - Results

Okay, so yes, complexity does help. But not the way you'd think.

Look at the accuracy column in the table. Let's see:

Logistic Regression: 85.4%
MLP: 88.2%
LSTM: 89.7%
Transformer: 90.3%

So the answer is: yes, complexity does equal better accuracy. Each step up the complexity ladder,
we get higher accuracy.

But—and this is important—look at how much we gain at each step. Going from Logistic Regression to MLP?
 2.8 percentage points. MLP to LSTM? 1.5 points. LSTM to Transformer? Only 0.6 points.

So yes, complexity helps. But the gains are getting smaller. That's the key insight. 
More complexity doesn't mean proportionally more accuracy, potentially due to dataset size being the limiting 
factor.

Look at those confusion matrices on the right. They tell the story.

Tthe black boxes right in the center of each matrix? That's neutral sentiment. 
The models got really good at predicting neutral. Why? Because 62.5% of the data is neutral. 
The models learned that's the safe prediction.

Now look at the edges—top left and bottom right. Those areas are much lighter.
That's negative and positive sentiment. The models made more mistakes there.

Look at the F1 scores: neutral gets 0.84 to 0.88, but negative sentiment only hits 0.81 to 0.82.
That's the imbalance showing up in the metrics.

So here's the answer to this question: Yes, bias and imbalance heavily affect performance. 
The models are biased toward the majority class because that's what's dominant in the data.
 But that bias also means they're worse at the minority classes.

One more thing—look at the off-diagonal errors. Some headlines were positive but got labeled neutral.
Some were negative but got called neutral. That's the implicit sentiment problem. 
Headlines describing strengths without explicit growth language confuse the model. It defaults to neutral.

Now look at the bottom of the table: training time.

Logistic Regression: 0.057 minutes
MLP: 32.578 minutes
LSTM: 32.602 minutes
Transformer: 35.226 minutes

So Logistic Regression trains in less than a tenth of a minute. The neural models? 32 to 35 minutes. 
That's 570 to 620 times longer.

Now the question is asking: is training time less important than accuracy? Or should we trade compute 
for performance?

Here's the honest answer: it depends.

If you need the best possible accuracy and you can afford the compute time?
 Then Transformer at 90.3% is worth the extra 35 minutes.

But if you're thinking practically? MLP gets you 88.2% accuracy in 32.6 minutes. 
That's pretty damn good. For most real-world applications, that might be enough. 
And it's only 570 times longer than Logistic Regression—versus Transformer at 620 times longer.

Or if you need speed above all else? Logistic Regression is done in seconds.

So the real answer is: Don't automatically trade accuracy for speed or vice versa. 
Think about your actual requirements. A 0.6% jump in accuracy (LSTM to Transformer)
doesn't justify 3% more training time for everyone.

Slide 6 - Limitations + Future Work

Before I wrap up, I want to be real about the limitations because they're important.

Limitation one: Data imbalance.
62.5% neutral skews everything. All models learned to exploit this. 
They're better at neutral but worse at the hard classes. That's realistic,
but it means minority class performance suffers. If you're trying to catch negative sentiment, 
these models will miss stuff.

Limitation two: I only trained each model once.
Yeah, I know. With computational constraints, I couldn't run multiple training sessions with 
different random seeds. So I can't say definitively whether differences are real or just random. 
Ideally I'd do 5 runs and report confidence intervals.

Limitation three: I used frozen FinBERT.
I didn't fine-tune it on my data. That was a practical choice—saves time and memory, 
and it prevented overfitting on imbalanced data. But it meant the model couldn't adapt to
the specific quirks of financial news. Fine-tuning could probably increase accuracy. 
Is it worth the extra training time? That's a trade-off.

But here's the good news: these limitations point to exactly what should happen next.

To improve the dataset imbalance, techniques like data augmentation or synthetic data generation
could help. Or collecting more negative sentiment headlines.

For the training variability, running multiple experiments with different seeds would give
more robust results, allowing for the calculation of confidence intervals and statistical significance testing.

And finally, fine-tuning FinBERT on this specific dataset could unlock better performance,
allowing the model to better capture the nuances of financial news headlines.
